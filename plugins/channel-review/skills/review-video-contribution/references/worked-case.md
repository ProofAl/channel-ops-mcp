# Worked example for a fictional course channel

The team needs to decide whether to repeat its tutorial. The reporting window is closed. The ten paid orders were all for $100. Four used a code unique to the tutorial. Three buyers later named YouTube on a form. Two of the three also used the code. Nobody named a specific video on the form. Source is missing for all other orders.

One coded order was refunded in full. Fulfillment and payment fees were $10 on **each** paid order, including the refund. Production cost $30 cash plus four hours at a loaded $35 per hour. Promotion cost $60. In this example, all campaign spending belongs to the tutorial.

| Evidence after overlap removal | Paid orders | Gross cash | Refunds | Fulfillment and fees |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Tutorial code | 4 | $400 | $100 | $40 |
| Channel reported only | 1 | $100 | $0 | $10 |
| Unknown source | 5 | $500 | $0 | $50 |
| All orders | 10 | $1,000 | $100 | $100 |

Campaign spending was $30 + 4 × $35 + $60 = $230. Tutorial-linked contribution was $400 − $100 − $40 − $230 = **$30**. The channel-only buyer adds $90 of contribution before campaign spending. Combining these under a broader channel scenario gives **$120** after the same $230 campaign spend. That scenario does not establish the tutorial generated $120.

Repeat the tutorial as a small test at a comparable cost. Use a distinct release code again. On subsequent calls, record whether the buyer can name the specific episode. The current $30 linked surplus would become a $50 loss if the next release cost another $80 and order economics stayed the same. This case demonstrates calculation and attribution using invented inputs.
